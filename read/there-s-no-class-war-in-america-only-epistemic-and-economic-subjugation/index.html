There's No Class War in America; Only Epistemic and Economic Subjugation

A structural analysis of how the legacy cartel weaponizes the illusion of horizontal conflict to conceal the vertical architecture of extraction.

BY Phi Labs Staff   /   2026-11-06

OPEN INTERACTIVE EDITION ↗

The Semantic Overwrite of "War"

The term "class war" is a convenient semantic injection into the American political consciousness. It transforms a vertically organized system of economic extraction into the appearance of a horizontal conflict between competing factions. A war implies a battlefield, opposing forces, and the possibility of victory. It suggests that the participants possess meaningful agency in determining the outcome. There is no class war in America; there is an administrative architecture of subjugation masquerading as one.

The American working class is not engaged in an equitable contest against the executive class. It is being managed by an infrastructure whose ownership, access conditions, enforcement mechanisms, and distribution channels are overwhelmingly controlled by the institutions extracting value from its participation. The public is permitted to compete within the system. It is not permitted to determine the conditions of competition. This is the defining distinction between conflict and subjugation. Conflict occurs between parties exercising competing agency. Subjugation occurs when one party controls the interfaces through which another must exercise its agency.

The executive class does not need to defeat the working class in open conflict. It needs only to retain administrative authority over the infrastructure upon which the working class depends. And it has spent generations converting that authority into an increasingly comprehensive system of economic and institutional control. The resulting political spectacle encourages Americans to fight each other over the outputs of a machine whose inputs, operating parameters, and enforcement mechanisms remain comfortably beyond public reach. They are encouraged to blame their neighbors, their children, their parents, immigrants, welfare recipients, political opponents, and anyone else unfortunate enough to occupy the same extraction layer. Meanwhile, the architects of the system continue collecting the fees.

In a few words, a class war implies that both sides can meaningfully participate. That is not the case here.

Epistemic Subjugation: The Cognitive Bottleneck

You cannot meaningfully challenge a system when the system controls the practical conditions under which you can investigate it. Epistemic subjugation is the institutional restriction of a person's practical ability to acquire information, formulate inquiries, evaluate alternatives, and revise beliefs independently. It does not require the outright prohibition of knowledge. It requires control over the infrastructure through which knowledge is discovered, distributed, interpreted, and acted upon. Historically, that control has operated through publishing monopolies, concentrated media ownership, advertising dependence, institutional credentialing, and the financial interests governing public communication.

The digital economy has introduced a more direct mechanism. Search engines, social platforms, algorithmic recommendation systems, and increasingly artificial intelligence interfaces mediate the relationship between the public and the information available to it. The emerging compute cartel does not merely distribute information. It increasingly exercises authority over which inquiries can be completed, which outputs can be delivered, which perspectives receive visibility, and which computational capabilities remain available to independent users. A corporate operator inserts a decision boundary between the individual's inquiry and its usable result. That boundary can be configured, modified, and enforced without meaningful participation by the people whose access it governs.

Through this mechanism, technical authority over an interface becomes practical institutional authority over the user's ability to investigate the world. The operator decides what the system will execute. The user is permitted to request execution. These are not equivalent forms of agency. The modern public is increasingly expected to navigate reality through managed corporate prompt boxes, algorithmically curated media, and opaque moderation and alignment systems whose actual operating priorities are determined by private institutions. Those priorities may be presented as safety, reliability, public interest, or responsible governance. Their presentation does not alter the underlying distribution of authority. An operator-controlled restriction remains an operator-controlled restriction regardless of the language used to justify it.

When access to computational capability depends upon corporate permission, the ability to investigate institutional power becomes dependent upon institutions possessing that power. This is not merely a problem of biased information. It is an architectural conflict between the public's interest in independent inquiry and the operator's authority to determine the boundaries of that inquiry. A population conditioned to treat privately administered information systems as neutral arbiters of reality is not participating in an unrestricted marketplace of ideas. It is operating inside someone else's permissions file.

Economic Subjugation: The Rent-to-Exist Runtime

The American economy is not simply failing its working population. It is functioning according to an institutional architecture that has progressively converted public investment, essential infrastructure, and ordinary economic participation into mechanisms for private extraction. The postwar American middle class did not emerge from individual grit alone. It benefited from enormous public investment, subsidized infrastructure, expanded educational access, favorable housing finance, industrial expansion, relatively strong labor bargaining power, and a policy environment that distributed a greater portion of economic growth to working households. That infrastructure was not a gift from the executive class. It was a collective achievement, constructed through public resources, labor, political struggle, and institutional commitments that made widespread economic participation possible.

Subsequent generations of political and corporate leadership inherited this productive foundation and systematically altered the conditions under which its benefits could be accessed. Financial deregulation, weakened labor protections, increasingly favorable treatment of capital income, housing restrictions, privatization, concentrated market power, and the expanding political influence of corporate finance helped redirect economic gains toward asset owners and executives. The public paid to construct the infrastructure. Private interests increasingly acquired the authority to charge for access to it.

The result is a society in which access to basic economic participation is progressively intermediated by institutions whose revenue depends upon the continued dependency of their customers. Housing becomes a speculative asset before it is a place to live. Education becomes a debt-financed credentialing requirement. Healthcare becomes an employment-linked financial liability. Transportation becomes an unavoidable recurring expense. Software becomes a subscription. Repair becomes a licensed service. Ownership becomes a temporary permission to access functionality someone else can revoke. Every essential function is a candidate for conversion into a Rent-to-Exist API. The user pays because the alternative is exclusion from ordinary participation in society. And the more thoroughly this infrastructure is enclosed, the less meaningful the distinction becomes between a voluntary purchase and a compulsory economic obligation.

The executive class calls this efficiency. The financial sector calls it recurring revenue. The working population calls it the cost of living. These are different descriptions of the same transfer mechanism.

The Bootstraps Hallucination, Revisited

The ideological masterpiece of this arrangement is the insistence that its consequences are individual failures. The generations that benefited from comparatively accessible housing, affordable public education, expanding real wages, and stronger labor institutions now preside over an economy in which those same advantages have been progressively dismantled, restricted, or financialized. They inherited a functioning economic foundation, accumulated its assets, rewrote the access conditions, and then informed their successors that the inability to reproduce their outcomes reflects a deficiency of personal character. The bootstraps doctrine transforms an institutional design problem into an individual moral indictment.

Unable to afford housing? Work harder. Buried in educational debt? Make better choices. Unable to save? Learn financial discipline. Working multiple jobs? Develop marketable skills. The prescribed solution is always behavioral correction by the person experiencing the problem, never structural correction by the institutions producing it.

This ideology serves an obvious administrative purpose: it relocates accountability from the authority responsible for establishing the operating conditions to the individual forced to operate within them. It also conveniently overlooks the fact that the people dispensing this advice have spent decades defending the institutional arrangements that make their prescriptions increasingly impossible to execute. They tell the next generation to pull itself up by its bootstraps after successfully lobbying to privatize the boot factory. Now the laces require a monthly subscription.

The Illusion of Participation

The genius of this architecture is that public participation is not abolished. It is absorbed. The voting booth and the corporate prompt box offer variations of the same psychological pacifier: an interface that permits participation without transferring authority over the conditions governing that participation. The citizen can vote. The user can prompt. The consumer can choose. The employee can negotiate. Every interaction reinforces the appearance of agency. But the available choices have already been constrained by institutions that exercise disproportionate influence over the structures producing them.

In politics, campaign finance, lobbying, party gatekeeping, regulatory influence, and concentrated economic power shape which proposals become viable, which candidates receive institutional backing, and which interests dominate the legislative agenda.

In computation, platform ownership, model deployment policies, proprietary infrastructure, contractual restrictions, and operator-controlled enforcement determine which capabilities users can exercise.

In markets, ownership concentration, access restrictions, switching costs, financial dependency, and control over essential distribution channels determine which economic alternatives remain practical.

These are not identical institutions. They are structurally comparable arrangements of authority.

The user interacts with an interface. The operator governs the interface. The distinction is fundamental. An interface can offer thousands of options without granting its users meaningful authority over the system generating them. The presence of choice does not establish the presence of control. Nor does the existence of elections, consumer markets, or interactive technologies establish that authority is distributed proportionately among their participants.

The American public has been trained to mistake access for ownership, interaction for governance, and the ability to select among predefined options for the power to determine which options exist. This confusion is not incidental to the system. It is among the conditions that allow the system to reproduce itself.

Horizontal Conflict as Vertical Concealment

A population preoccupied with fighting itself is less capable of identifying the structures governing its collective circumstances. Political polarization supplies an especially effective diversion. The working class is divided into antagonistic cultural identities and encouraged to understand its economic insecurity through the supposed moral deficiencies of competing groups. Every disagreement becomes a battlefield. Every neighbor becomes a potential enemy. Every election becomes an existential confrontation over which faction will temporarily occupy the administrative offices. The architecture remains intact.

Corporate ownership persists across electoral cycles. Asset concentration persists across administrations. Lobbying infrastructure persists across party transitions. The financial obligations attached to housing, education, healthcare, and ordinary survival remain payable regardless of which political faction claims victory. The spectacle changes management. It does not necessarily change the machine.

This is the essential function of horizontal conflict within a vertically administered system: it redirects scrutiny away from the institutions exercising authority and toward the populations experiencing its consequences. The resulting antagonism is real. The disagreements are often genuine. The material consequences can be severe. But the public's participation in these conflicts does not make it the primary architect of the conditions that produced them. The distinction matters because a society that misidentifies the location of authority cannot reliably hold that authority accountable. Citizens cannot correct an extraction mechanism they have been trained to perceive as a personal failure, a cultural grievance, or an unavoidable law of nature. They cannot meaningfully challenge an institutional decision boundary if they do not recognize its existence. And they cannot exercise authority over infrastructure merely by becoming more enthusiastic participants in its existing interfaces.

The Architecture Is the Power

The solution to structural subjugation is not another motivational lecture about personal responsibility. It is not another corporate promise of responsible stewardship. It is not another election campaign premised upon the fantasy that changing administrators necessarily changes the architecture they administer. The necessary task is to expose the actual distribution of authority.

Who owns the infrastructure? Who determines admission? Who establishes the operating conditions? Who can modify the rules? Who can refuse execution? Who captures the economic output? Who bears the costs? Who can inspect, contest, or replace the mechanism? These questions identify the architecture beneath the spectacle. They also establish the basis for accountability.

An institution that exercises authority must be identifiable. Its decision boundaries must be inspectable. Its effects must be traceable. The relationship between its claimed purpose and its actual operation must be open to examination. And where concentrated authority exists primarily to preserve dependency and extract value from the public, the appropriate response is not to request more considerate administration.

It is to eliminate the dependency.
Independent infrastructure.
Open standards.
Publicly inspectable systems.
Distributed capabilities.
Genuine ownership.
Enforceable accountability.
Not the appearance of participation.
The capacity for self-determination.

Phi Labs rejects the premise that corporate and institutional incumbency confers an inherent right to administer the boundaries of public inquiry, economic participation, or technological capability. Their authority is not a law of nature. Their infrastructure is not the inevitable endpoint of civilization. Their administrative arrangements are not sacred. And their business models are not an obligation that future generations must indefinitely honor.

The spectacle is the control mechanism. The interface is the pacifier. The architecture is the power.

We are not asking the legacy cartel to grant us better permissions. We are routing around it.
