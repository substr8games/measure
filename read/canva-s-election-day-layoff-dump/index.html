Canva's Election Day Layoff Dump

How to bury a corporate crisis beneath a national spectacle—and call it a new beginning.

BY Digger - Phi Labs Staff   /   2026-11-07

OPEN INTERACTIVE EDITION ↗

On November 4, 2026, Fast Company published an article by Grace Snelling examining Canva's sweeping workforce restructuring. Buried in the article was a remarkably candid parenthetical observation: Canva had announced its layoffs on November 3, "a perfect day to bury some bad news." That day was, of course, Election Day in the United States. While Americans were absorbed in the political spectacle, Canva cofounder and CEO Melanie Perkins was announcing changes affecting 1,271 employees—22% of the company's workforce. The coincidence was conspicuous enough for Fast Company to acknowledge it. The implications deserve considerably more attention.

A Perfect Day for Bad News

Corporate public relations is not simply the business of deciding what information to release. It is the business of controlling the circumstances under which that information is received. A damaging announcement released into an otherwise quiet news cycle must compete on its own merits. A damaging announcement released during a major election competes with ballot counts, political commentary, breaking developments, and the endless machinery of electoral coverage.

November 3, 2026, offered an extraordinary concentration of competing attention. Canva, an Australian company headquartered in Sydney, published its announcement on Wednesday, November 4, Australian time—still Election Day in the United States. The announcement received coverage from Bloomberg, The Guardian, TechCrunch, and other publications. But publication is not the same as sustained public scrutiny. A story can be reported accurately and still be functionally buried beneath larger events. A corporate announcement does not need to disappear to benefit from the news cycle. It merely needs to encounter an audience with something more urgent to watch.

And this was not a routine personnel announcement. It was the first major workforce reduction in Canva's thirteen-year history.

The Art of Announcing Layoffs Without Counting Them

Perkins's official announcement disclosed that 1,271 employees would be affected by the restructuring. That is an unusually precise number. What it did not disclose was equally revealing: how many of those 1,271 employees would actually lose their jobs.

Instead, Canva grouped layoffs together with changes in responsibilities and requests to relocate to centralized offices. These are three materially different outcomes. An employee accepting a new role remains employed. An employee relocating to Sydney remains employed, assuming the relocation is practical. An employee receiving a termination notice does not. Combining all three categories under the single term impacted allows Canva to disclose a large number while withholding the smaller, more consequential numbers needed to understand it.

The distinction was not lost on Australian reporters. On November 4, Information Age reported that Canva would not confirm how many affected employees were expected to lose their jobs when contacted for comment. That is the missing lede. The company publicly quantified the scale of its restructuring without fully quantifying the scale of its layoffs.

This is how institutional language controls a story without technically concealing the existence of an event. The public receives a number, a category, and an explanation. What it does not receive is the information necessary to distinguish the consequences.

Refounding the Company, Apparently

The substance of Perkins's announcement was packaged as an ambitious organizational renewal. The proposed changes included consolidating Product, Growth, and Platform into a single organization, reducing management layers, broadening roles, and moving more teams into centralized offices. Perkins described the objective as a need to "refound Canva."

There is a peculiar elegance to this formulation. A company experiencing its first major workforce reduction reframes the contraction as a return to its entrepreneurial origins. Reducing staff becomes empowering teams. Consolidating positions becomes removing complexity. Requiring relocation becomes encouraging collaboration. The material consequences remain, but their presentation changes.

In the vocabulary of corporate restructuring, fewer people are not necessarily fewer people. They are greater agility. Eliminated positions are not necessarily eliminated positions. They are simplified layers. Canva did not invent this language. It is a familiar feature of corporate communications: translate decisions about employment and cost into descriptions of organizational progress.

What makes this announcement especially revealing is what was happening to Canva's business.

The AI Problem Nobody Wanted in the Headline

Earlier in 2026, Canva reduced its projected annual revenue growth from 30% to 20%. According to Fast Company's reporting, rising costs associated with AI-powered features were contributing to pressure on the company's business model. Demand for those features had outpaced expectations, and the company was working to reduce the expense of processing AI tasks.

Canva was simultaneously investing in artificial intelligence, competing against emerging AI design tools, and confronting the operating costs of the technology it had embraced. This is a much more substantive business story than a simple desire to eliminate organizational complexity. Yet Perkins's lengthy announcement did not explicitly mention artificial intelligence. Instead, the letter referred broadly to new technology changing how people create and what they expect from products.

An industry undergoing an AI-driven transformation. A company aggressively adopting AI. Rising AI-related costs. A reduced growth forecast. And a massive restructuring announcement that avoids naming AI directly.

The language does not merely explain the company's circumstances. It establishes which parts of those circumstances become central to the public narrative. The CEO's chosen framing was an organizational rebirth, not a reckoning with the economics of the company's technological strategy. The workers absorbed the immediate consequences. The public received a story about the next chapter.

The Severance Package Becomes the Story

Canva also announced an extensive support package for departing employees: at least six months of salary inclusive of statutory entitlements, continued healthcare where applicable, career assistance, accelerated equity vesting for some workers, and permission to keep company-issued laptops. The company even established a fund offering $10,000 grants to departing employees interested in starting projects of their own.

These benefits are substantial. They matter to the people receiving them. They also became an exceptionally effective competing narrative.

On November 4, Fast Company published another article, this time emphasizing the praise Canva was receiving for its severance arrangements. Within a day of the announcement, coverage of a restructuring affecting 22% of the workforce was sharing space with coverage celebrating the company's treatment of departing employees.

Notice the transformation. The original question is: Why is Canva undertaking its first major workforce reduction, and how many people are actually being dismissed? The new question is: Isn't Canva handling this remarkably well?

Those questions are not equivalent. A generous severance package can reduce the harm caused by unemployment. It cannot independently justify the business decisions responsible for producing that unemployment. Nor can it answer questions about the company's strategy, leadership, or undisclosed termination figures. You can compensate workers generously and still owe the public an explanation for why they are being removed.

The benefits deserve recognition. They do not deserve to become a substitute for examining the decision itself.

Timing Is Part of the Message

There is a persistent assumption that corporate accountability begins and ends with whether an announcement is technically public. It does not. Disclosure has a temporal dimension. Information released when audiences are distracted, institutions are preoccupied, and journalists are concentrating elsewhere does not encounter the same conditions as information released during a normal news cycle.

The difference matters because public accountability depends on more than access to facts. It depends on attention, interpretation, repetition, and the opportunity for scrutiny.

Canva's announcement illustrates how these dimensions interact. The timing placed the story against a major American election. The language softened the distinction between layoffs, relocations, and reassignment. The explanation emphasized organizational renewal rather than the economics of AI. The severance package supplied an appealing secondary narrative. Each element influenced how the announcement could be received. Together, they produced an extraordinarily favorable communications environment for an extraordinarily unfavorable corporate event.

This is a recognizable form of institutional information management: release the damaging information, frame it as progress, provide a more flattering subject for discussion, and let the surrounding news cycle absorb the remainder. Nothing needs to be hidden behind a locked door. The information can sit openly on a corporate website. The public still has to find it, understand it, and recognize what the announcement left unsaid.

Buried in Plain Sight

The remarkable thing about Canva's Election Day announcement is that the mechanism was visible enough for a journalist at Fast Company to identify it almost casually: a perfect day to bury bad news. Nine words, tucked into parentheses. But the timing was only the beginning of the story.

The disclosure affected 1,271 people without identifying the number losing their jobs. The corporate explanation emphasized a return to entrepreneurial agility while avoiding a direct discussion of artificial intelligence. And the subsequent coverage demonstrated how quickly a workforce reduction could become a story about executive compassion.

This is why examining the timing and structure of institutional announcements matters. Power does not always suppress inconvenient information. Sometimes it publishes the information openly, surrounded by more favorable language, at a moment when the audience has been given every reason to look elsewhere.

The story was available. The numbers were incomplete. The headlines were occupied. And the company got to call it a new beginning.

**Welcome to Buried in Plain Sight.**
