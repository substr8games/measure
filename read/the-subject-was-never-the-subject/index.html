The Subject Was Never the Subject

How Dan Olson (Folding Ideas) uses the visible absurdities of the internet to expose the structures that produce them.

BY Phi Labs Staff   /   2026-11-08

OPEN INTERACTIVE EDITION ↗

Featured Figures #02 — Dan Olson / Folding Ideas

The internet is full of things that are easy to ridicule and surprisingly difficult to explain. Cryptocurrency millionaires buying cartoon monkeys. People insisting the Earth is flat. Investors paying enormous sums for virtual real estate in a video game nobody seems particularly interested in playing. Any of these subjects could sustain an entertaining documentary. Their contradictions are obvious enough to invite mockery, and their participants frequently provide all the comedy anyone could reasonably ask for.

But Dan Olson, the Canadian documentarian behind the YouTube channel Folding Ideas, has made something of a specialty out of asking a more interesting question: What kind of system produces this behavior, and why does that behavior make sense within it?

His investigations often begin with a recognizable cultural curiosity and end somewhere considerably larger. The apparent subject becomes an entry point into economic incentives, institutional relationships, ideological commitments, and the mechanisms through which people construct their understanding of reality. Olson does not simply replace an amusing subject with a more serious one. He follows the relationships between them until the distinction becomes difficult to maintain. Three of his documentaries demonstrate how that works.

The Future Is a Dead Mall

In March 2023, Olson published The Future Is a Dead Mall — Decentraland and the Metaverse, written with Nathan Landel. Decentraland was promoted as a new frontier of digital existence: a persistent environment where users could own property, operate businesses, attend events, and participate in the economic and social life of an emerging metaverse. The premise of Olson's investigation is almost irresistibly funny because the environment he encounters bears so little resemblance to its advertised future.

The world looks like an abandoned commercial development whose owners have been assured the customers are coming any day now. There are vacant spaces, awkward attractions, storefronts without compelling reasons to visit, and enormous quantities of virtual property awaiting their economic destiny. Olson spends time simply experiencing the place, allowing the gulf between its promotional promises and observable functionality to become increasingly absurd. It would have been easy to stop at the comedy of a disappointing video game.

But that would have mistaken the visible failure for the entire explanation. A conventional review might ask why Decentraland was not a better game. Olson's investigation invites a different question: Was making a good game ever the organizing priority?

Consider what makes a shared virtual environment worth inhabiting. People need reasons to visit, things to do, others to meet, and experiences that justify returning. A convincing virtual world needs sustained activity and participation. Now consider a system organized around the sale and ownership of scarce virtual land. Its investment proposition depends on an anticipated future in which property purchased today becomes more desirable tomorrow.

That expected future popularity creates present demand for assets before the popularity exists. The design priorities can pull in different directions: a social world needs useful, engaging places, while a speculative property market benefits from scarcity, tradability, and the belief that somebody else will eventually want what is being bought. A virtual shopping mall can contain purchasable property without becoming a place where anyone wants to shop. An economically legible asset is not necessarily a socially useful environment.

The contrast becomes even clearer when Olson examines governance. Decentraland's decentralized autonomous organization, or DAO, lets eligible asset holders vote on certain decisions. Yet, as Decentraland's own DAO guide specifies, voting weight is calculated from holdings including MANA, LAND, and NAMES. The platform decentralizes some decision procedures, but it does not distribute voting influence equally among its participants. Property ownership remains a configured route to authority.

Moving an administrative mechanism onto a blockchain does not, by itself, change the distribution of power. A voting interface can be technically decentralized while the authority graph it implements is weighted toward those who hold more assets. The question is not whether a blockchain is involved, but who can issue an effective decision, how much weight it receives, and which rules carry it into execution. Those are properties of the arrangement, not the promotional language describing it.

Evaluated against its promise of an engaging shared world, the Decentraland Olson toured appears deeply dysfunctional. Evaluated as a system in which a vision of future participation makes presently scarce assets marketable, many of its apparently strange priorities become understandable. The promised metaverse is not merely decoration around the property market; belief in that future helps make the present property market conceivable. That does not establish that every creator or investor was insincere. It establishes that the narrative itself performs an economic function.

The distinction is crucial: a platform can underperform as a place to live or play while still operating as an asset marketplace. The first loop rewards actual participation; the second can reward expectations about participation. Those loops may reinforce each other, but their success is not identical. Olson's empty plazas are therefore more revealing than a conventional review score: they put the product being advertised alongside the incentives under which it was constructed.

The dead mall is not merely a failure to achieve the promised future. It exposes the difference between the future being advertised and the system's incentives in the present. The dead mall is the visible result. The machinery that produced it is the subject.

Line Goes Up

Olson's earlier documentary, Line Goes Up — The Problem With NFTs, published in January 2022, performs a similar maneuver on a much larger scale. An NFT initially appears to be a technological object. What makes a token non-fungible? What does it mean to own one? How does a blockchain record a transfer, and what relationship does that record have to the digital thing it supposedly represents? Olson takes those questions seriously, but the documentary begins somewhere unexpected: the financial crisis of 2008.

The opening is not a digression. It provides an economic and ideological context for the promises later attached to cryptocurrency. From there, Olson examines the technical system, the trading markets, the promotional claims, and the incentives of participants who already hold cryptocurrency. The question gradually shifts from what an NFT can do to why those capabilities are offered as solutions and who stands to benefit when more people accept them.

Here the component becomes an interface. Seen alone, an NFT looks like an unusual entry in a database seeking a purpose. Seen within the speculative cryptocurrency market Olson describes, it also serves as an intake valve: a product that gives new buyers a reason to acquire the cryptocurrency needed to participate. His documentary makes this interpretation explicit in a chapter titled NFTs Exist To Get You To Buy Crypto. More incoming buyers can provide liquidity for existing holders looking to sell. The mechanism does not require every participant to be a fraud, nor does it imply that every cryptocurrency transaction operates the same way.

The technical capabilities can be real without proving the marketing story. A blockchain can record transactions. A unique token can be transferred. A program can execute the rules its developers wrote. None of those facts demonstrates that the assembled system grants the rights buyers believe they are purchasing or improves the position of the artists supposedly being served. The capability of a component and the consequence of connecting that component to a speculative market are different objects of inquiry.

That distinction is why Line Goes Up remains useful beyond the NFT boom. The documentary refuses to let technical vocabulary stand in for an account of incentives. It follows money, status, platform dependencies, and the expectation of resale through the system that gives the token its apparent significance. The important question is not merely whether the technology works. It is what the working technology is organized to accomplish.

In Search of a Flat Earth

The same investigative movement appears in In Search of a Flat Earth, published in September 2020. Olson opens with the apparently straightforward task of examining a false claim about the physical world. He investigates flat-Earth arguments and makes his own observations to show why their account of the planet fails. For a substantial portion of the documentary, the viewer could reasonably expect a rigorous, sometimes funny debunking of a very old misconception.

Then the film turns toward QAnon and the wider social function of conspiratorial explanation. What appeared to be a disagreement about geometry becomes an investigation of the communities, commitments, and explanatory habits that help certain beliefs survive correction. If the problem were only a lack of sufficiently convincing evidence, then a better demonstration should settle it. But a belief can also serve as an admission rule for a community, a source of belonging, and a framework for assigning blame and meaning.

Once those functions are connected to a belief, replacing a factual claim need not dismantle the relationships that made it attractive. A cosmological story and a political conspiracy can be very different in content while offering some participants a familiar arrangement: insiders who see the truth, outsiders who are deceived, and a promised explanation for a world that feels hostile or incomprehensible. That is the component swap worth investigating. The documentary does not need to establish that every flat-Earth believer migrated to QAnon for its larger point to hold.

This is not an argument against evidence, nor an assertion that everyone who believes a conspiracy theory has the same motivations. It is a distinction between correcting an assertion and explaining the social system in which the assertion circulates. If a claim helps maintain a boundary of belonging, a factual correction reaches only part of the mechanism. Olson's subject becomes not the curvature of the Earth, but the arrangement that makes certain explanations compelling and resilient.

What the Three Investigations Share

In The Future Is a Dead Mall, Olson begins with an unsatisfying virtual environment and traces the economic and governance arrangements around it. In Line Goes Up, he begins with a digital token and follows the market incentives that give it a role. In In Search of a Flat Earth, he begins with a false factual claim and examines the relationships that allow such claims to become elements of identity and community. The domains are different; the investigative movement is recognizable.

He establishes what is observable, examines the components and their interfaces, traces the rewards and constraints that guide behavior, and revises his account of the original object. The explanation moves from artifact to arrangement to consequence. Its power comes not from declaring that everything is connected, but from demonstrating which connections matter and what they permit or produce.

This resembles the reasoning formalized in Phi Labs' Structuralist Methodology. A system's behavior cannot always be inferred from the capabilities of its parts considered separately. How components are connected, who controls their interfaces, what crosses the boundaries, and where incentives feed back into decisions help determine what the assembled system actually does. A virtual land parcel is not a community. A token is not an economy. A mistaken belief is not, by itself, an explanation of a movement.

That approach also changes the question of intervention. Better graphics might improve a virtual plaza without changing the relationship between property speculation and participation. A more reliable NFT contract might execute transfers more predictably without changing the economic incentives of the market using it. A better flat-Earth debunk might defeat a specific factual assertion without changing the social relationships that reward another one. Repairing an individual component matters, but it does not guarantee that the larger behavior will change.

There is a useful irony here. Olson uses structural analysis to investigate conspiracy thinking without adopting conspiracy thinking as his explanatory method. The two approaches may share an intuition that something larger than individual choice is at work, but they differ in what counts as an explanation. Where a conspiracy narrative supplies unseen coordination, a structural account has to identify the observable incentives, decision rules, interfaces, and evidence that make a particular outcome possible. It cannot substitute the feeling that a system exists for a demonstration of how it operates.

This is also a disciplined alternative to blaming isolated individuals for every repeated failure. It is easy to select the most visible actor and treat their choices as the entire causal story. It is harder to show how institutions, rewards, and constraints shape the available choices, and harder still to demonstrate which change would alter the outcome. Structural explanation does not abolish individual responsibility; it prevents individual responsibility from being mistaken for a sufficient account of the system.

Olson's documentaries earn their generalizations through inspection. He reads documentation, tests software, follows financial claims, visits the environments under discussion, and examines arguments on their own terms. His firsthand exploration of Decentraland places advertised capabilities next to observable behavior. His close examination of NFTs distinguishes the token from the asset, right, or promise associated with it. His study of flat-Earth claims establishes their factual failures before asking what else they do for the people who hold them.

The result is an unusually important kind of public explanation: an argument that shows its work. The audience is not required to accept an abstract claim about power, speculation, or belief merely because the narrator sounds confident. It is given a sequence of connections it can inspect, question, and test. This is how a concrete case becomes a way to understand a larger structure without treating superficial resemblance as proof.

The Subject Was Never the Subject

An empty shopping mall is an unusual starting point for a discussion of economic authority. So is a cartoon monkey, or a man standing beside a lake attempting to prove that the planet is flat. Their absurdity draws attention, but Olson's work does not depend on laughing at them. It depends on following each one into the arrangement that makes it intelligible. By the time the explanation reaches its larger object, the audience has already encountered the working parts.

That is why these documentaries remain useful after their immediate cultural moments pass. Decentraland's 2023 incarnation illustrates the difference between a speculative asset market and an engaging social environment. The NFT boom offers a case in which a technological capability became attached to a much larger financial proposition. The journey from flat Earth to QAnon reveals how a change in the subject of belief may leave some underlying social functions intact. Each case remains distinct, but the investigative discipline travels.

Dan Olson's achievement is not simply that he finds ridiculous things on the internet and demonstrates why they are ridiculous. It is that he follows them until the systems producing their contradictions become inspectable. Visibility does not guarantee protection from manipulation, but it changes the conditions under which manipulation operates: an arrangement that can be recognized, described, and challenged is harder to disguise as a natural fact of life.

Once the machinery is visible, the machine becomes less exploitable.
